OTOS Continuity™ · a private briefing for Public Health

Treat the driver upstream,watch the cost chain collapse downstream.

Based on my own experience, I believe addiction must be viewed through an ADHD lens.

There is a significant, high-cost, identifiable group of adults where — as with me — untreated ADHD is very likely driving, recycling and fanning the fire of the addiction. The chaos, the crises and the costs are just what arrives in view.

The intervention is getting that driver found and treated far earlier. Continuity is the non-clinical layer that holds a person in the days between the services already treating them — and it carries the whole thing: from the intervention through to a brain that is finally treated properly and can find its own way Out The Other Side. The way I did, without what it cost me to get there.

This is a plan to stop what happened to me happening to the next person, and I believe it takes a great deal of money out of this system. The method is the same for any complex cohort: identify the person far earlier, upstream; hold them through every handoff; and act before repeat chaos becomes repeat crisis, consequence and cost.

The national anchor is not mine and does not need to be. The independent ADHD Taskforce, reporting to NHS England, put the economic cost to individuals and government at "at least £17 billion" a year and named the drivers: unemployment, crime, substance misuse.

Added 9 Sep · the Taskforce, in full — TL-0087

The sentence is worth reading whole, because of the order it is written in. NHS England’s own independent taskforce, in the Abbreviated Summary of Part 1 and again in Part 2, identical wording in both:

ADHD, when unsupported, is a potent route into educational failure, long-term unemployment, crime, substance misuse, suicide, mental and physical illness. Economic costs to individuals and the government of at least £17 billion are avoidable as, when appropriately supported, people with ADHD can thrive and fully engage in a working life.

That is my life, in the order it happened. Educational failure. Long-term unemployment — I am on Universal Credit now. Crime: a drink-driving conviction and probation. Substance misuse. A suicide attempt. Two ICU admissions. I did not choose that list. It is NHS England’s, and it is in their own report, in that sequence.

Where the £17 billion comes from, and what the Taskforce says about it

Part 1, §3, “What are the costs of not treating ADHD?”: using Danish patient registry data, the cost difference between adults with ADHD and their same-sex siblings was approximately £17,000 more per adult with ADHD per year, and the UK estimate of £17 billion is that figure extrapolated at a prevalence of 2.5%. The underlying study is cited in the report as Daley, Jacobsen, Lange, Sørensen and Walldorf (2019), European Psychiatry 61:41–48.

And on the same page the Taskforce says this: “No published papers on the contemporary costs of untreated ADHD in the UK exist yet.”

I would rather you heard that from me. It is a Danish-derived extrapolation and the report says so itself. On the only published estimate available is a more honest sentence than a bare number, and it is the one I will keep using.

Added 8 Sep · the justice ring

And one part of that spend you know better than I do. NICE names it directly: people with a history of substance misuse, and people known to the criminal justice system, are both listed as groups with increased ADHD prevalence — NG87, recommendation 1.2.1. On release the continuity gap is measured: 57% of adults leaving secure settings start community treatment within three weeks, so roughly four in ten do not — OHID, secure settings 2024/25 — and weeks one and two after release carry a 7.5× relative risk of drug-related death against weeks three to twelve — Merrall et al, Addiction 2010.

What I am not going to claim: that ADHD medication reduces reoffending. The Swedish finding is observational, and the UK randomised trial was negative — Asherson et al, CIAO-II, BJPsych 2022. The argument is identification, assessment and continuity. Not medication.

Added 8 Sep · the HIU discovery — missing from this page entirely

Then I read NHS England’s own HIU evaluation.

NHS England commissioned a national evaluation of the High Intensity Use service. The Strategy Unit delivered it in February 2022 — 117 pages, five case-study sites, around a hundred HIU services operating across England.

On page 113 there is a service user. He is 28. The report calls him James.

“I tried to get a diagnosis for ADHD about 3 years ago. I kept calling for months and then it just went cold.”
“The doctors don’t really ask what you’re like… They couldn’t put two and two together.”

Those are the only two mentions of ADHD in the entire evaluation. Both are his. Neither reaches the findings, the themes or the recommendations. The report names what characterises this cohort in real detail — mental health running through virtually everybody, social isolation, homelessness, and alcohol use and self-medication, explicitly. It names the behaviour. It does not name the condition most associated with it.

The information existed. Someone wrote it down. It never reached the place where decisions get made.

TL-0290 + TL-0419 · 9 Sep · what the guidance actually says

“Grounded in” was the wrong phrase, and one half of the line should not have been there at all. The guidance is real, it is useful, and it is better cited precisely than claimed loosely. This is the accurate version:

  • NICE NG87, recommendation 1.2.1 names people with a history of substance misuse, and people known to the criminal justice system, as groups with increased ADHD prevalence. Recommendation 1.4.3 uses the words substance misuse and self-medication.
  • RCPsych CR235, s11.1 records that substance misuse “may be twice as common among individuals with ADHD as in the general population and tends to begin earlier”, and that individuals with untreated ADHD “may also describe self-medicating with illicit substances”.
  • Neither body says what to do about the overlap inside an addiction service. That is the question this asks.

And the two things CR235 also says, which I would rather raise myself: it recommends stabilising substance misuse first, and it says stimulants are not routinely recommended in chaotic misuse. Those are real constraints on any priority-review route, and they belong in the design, not in the small print.
