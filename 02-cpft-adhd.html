01

Who they are, and what they hold

The clinical route. There is no other one.

The CPFT Adult ADHD Team is the NHS assessment and treatment route for adult ADHD in this county. They own the only door that leads to the thing that actually changed my life.

This is not a criticism of that team. It is the clearest case I have for why catching ADHD earlier matters so much — and the reason the money argument works at all.

02

What happened instead

Seven years on a list, while the cost accumulated somewhere else.

My ADHD has been on a review list since 2019 — and while I cycled through A&E, addiction services and mental-health teams, the crisis loop continued. I am still waiting for my medication review today.

In April 2026 a consultant psychiatrist put the pattern in writing, in a letter addressed to this team. One sentence, and the order of it is everything:

“Adult ADHD, previously complicated with excess alcohol use to self-medicate inadequately managed ADHD symptoms.”

Independent consultant psychiatrist, Psymplicity Healthcare · April 2026, addressed to the CPFT Adult ADHD Team

Not “ADHD plus alcohol use disorder, treated separately.” Not “alcohol use disorder, with ADHD as comorbidity.” Adult ADHD, complicated by self-medication. The ADHD was the driver; the drinking was the visible symptom.

This letter is about my care. It is not proof that OTOS works, and it is not an endorsement of it. It is proof of something simpler: the driver underneath this kind of addiction is real, documented and, once assessed, clinically addressable.

03

Where the thread breaks

Before, and after.

This team has two breaks either side of it, and neither is theirs to fix alone.

  • Before. While someone waits, nobody is holding them. The wait is invisible to every other service — a person can be clinically known and still operationally lost.
  • After. The assessment happens, the treatment starts, and then the person leaves the room and the addiction side has no idea it happened. The assessment gets undone by a broken handoff.

That is what a continuity layer is for. Not a new service, not a new record — just enough for the addiction side to know the ADHD is being addressed, and for this team to know the person did not vanish the moment they left.

04

And the part nobody writes down

What it took to get past the list.

This is my experience and my opinion. It is not a complaint about the team, and it is not a boast.

In my experience, the only way I reached the right treatment was to go outside the NHS and pay for it myself — a private assessment, private titration, and the medication. I am still paying for it every month, today, while I remain on the NHS list.

I want to be careful about how that reads, because of what it says about who gets treated. I could find the money at the point I needed it. Most of the people I sat next to in addiction services could not, and cannot.

That is what I believe a waiting list of this length actually does. It does not delay everybody equally. It sorts people by what they can pay — and the ones who cannot pay are the ones already costing the system the most, in the most expensive places.

I did not recover by waiting my turn.

My own account and my own opinion. It is a statement about a waiting time, not about the clinicians on the other end of it.

05

What the thread would actually do

It sits around the person — not between the records.

This is the part that decides whether it is safe. It does not access, merge or write to any EPR. Rather than wiring services together, it carries a single consent-led continuity token around the individual. Each service interacts only with the person and with the token — never with each other’s notes. It is the same five-part mechanism the CGL page sets out — deliberately, because it is one thread, not one per service. What changes below is the last column.

What it isWhat it doesWhy it matters to the team
Micro check-insSimple signals between appointmentsShows whether someone is still connected, or starting to drift
Transition receiptsRecords whether a handoff happenedStops referrals to ADHD care disappearing into silence
Drift visibilityFlags missing follow-throughMakes relapse risk visible earlier, for the team to act
Human review queueKeeps decisions with peopleNo automated clinical decisions — clinical judgement stays central
Tokenised continuity trailHolds minimal continuity signalsJourney visibility without becoming a new clinical record

So the addiction service, this team and the wellbeing route can each see that follow-through is happening, and that the ADHD is being addressed, without ever seeing inside one another’s systems.

And specifically, what it gives the ADHD team

Their process is untouched. What changes is what it is worth:

  • A bounded group is put forward for review by mutual agreement with the addiction services — their clinical decision, their criteria, every time.
  • Once they assess and treat, the person stays visible and connected rather than lost to a broken handoff.
  • The waiting list stops being invisible to everyone else — the services carrying the cost of it can see that the person is being held.
“Not joining up the services — holding the person across them.”
06

What I would ask of them

A conversation, and their criteria.

Not approval. Not a pilot. Not new responsibility.

  • Their clinical eyes on who would safely sit in — and out of — a small, jointly agreed group.
  • Whether a clinically governed priority-review route, subject to their criteria and their governance, could let this be tested properly.
  • Nothing about their assessment process changes. The question is only whether it can be made to count earlier, for the people most likely to benefit.
07

Why this one is different for you

It is the only service on this list that is not yours.

CPFT sits with the ICB, not the Council. That matters for the route question — and it is one of the three things I would value your view on.

But the cost of their waiting list does not land on the ICB. It lands on drug and alcohol treatment, on A&E, on housing, on police and courts — on budget lines you can see and some you carry.

That is the argument in one line: the cost does not disappear. It moves to a worse budget line.

And there is one more thing that makes that wait hard to look at. The guideline this team works to already names the group I was in — not as an aside, but as a numbered recommendation:

NICE, on where to look

“People with a history of substance misuse — and people known to the criminal justice system — are named as groups with increased ADHD prevalence.”

NICE NG87, recommendation 1.2.1

Their own guideline already tells them where the density is. I reached this team through both of those doors and it made no difference to the wait.

What NG87 does not say, and I will not pretend it does: it sets no sequencing condition, and across all 61 pages the word alcohol does not appear once. It names the groups. It does not say what to do about the overlap inside an addiction service. That is the gap, and naming it as a gap is the argument.

And as for what that wait costs while it runs, the one published per-person estimate comes from NHS England’s own ADHD Taskforce. It is a per-person figure, not a system one:

~£17,000

additional public spending per adult with ADHD, per year, compared with their same-sex siblings

Report of the independent ADHD Taskforce, NHS England — Part 1, 20 June 2025. Danish registry data, sibling-controlled

Three caveats, stated before you ask. It is Danish registry data, not UK. It is a sibling comparison, not a whole-system cost. And it describes adults with ADHD — not adults with ADHD and addiction, who would plausibly cost more, not less. That is an argument to make. It is not a number I will assert.
